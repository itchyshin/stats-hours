Is the model any good?

A residual plot tells you whether the model is embarrassed by the data you have. Simulating from the model tells you what the model believes about data you have not seen, and that is where the embarrassing beliefs live.

Objectives

By the end of this class you should be able to:

  1. Say what a response, a Pearson and a randomised quantile residual each divide by, why all three coincide in a Gaussian model, and why only the third can be judged against the same picture in every family.
  2. Read a worm plot: say what is on each axis, what a correct model looks like, and what the envelope drawn round it does and does not promise.
  3. Calibrate a diagnostic by simulating its own null rather than by eye, and say how many points outside an envelope is a lot.
  4. Check a model by simulating from it: draw new data with an explicit generator, recompute a statistic you care about, and place the observed value in the simulated distribution — and say why a check you pass is much weaker than a check you fail.

Three residuals, and why you were only taught one

Itchy

Toto’s model first, because it is the one where nothing goes wrong.

Pearson residual SD         : 1.0029

Itchy

Three names, three definitions, one vector. The response residual is observed minus fitted, in millimetres. The Pearson residual divides that by the standard deviation the family decrees for that row — here σ, the same 1.413 mm for every bird, so it is just a change of units. The randomised quantile residual asks the fitted model what probability it puts below the value you actually saw and maps that probability through a normal.

Toto

And the last two are the same number.

Itchy

To 3e-14 millimetres, which is arithmetic noise and not a coincidence. In a Gaussian model the response is normal by assumption, so mapping it into a normal and back does nothing at all. In the one model everybody is taught, all three residuals are the same object. That is exactly why “the residual” feels like one word, and it is why the habits you brought from that model do not survive contact with a count.

Momo

So do it to mine.

Itchy

Same three definitions, and this time write out what each one divides by, because the family has an opinion now. The engine does not offer a Pearson residual, and that is no loss: writing (y − μ̂)/√V(μ̂) yourself forces you to say which variance function the family decreed, which is Class 3’s whole lesson turned into arithmetic.

pois = drm(bf(@formula(Fledglings ~ Age)), Poisson(); data = females)

mu_p = fitted(pois)
r_response = residuals(pois; type = :response)
# Poisson decrees Var(y) = mu, so the Pearson divisor is sqrt(mu), row by row.
r_pearson = r_response ./ sqrt.(mu_p)
r_quantile = residuals(pois; type = :quantile, rng = MersenneTwister(1))

for (nm, r) in (("response", r_response), ("Pearson", r_pearson), ("quantile", r_quantile))

Toto

All three are four columns, because age only takes four values. But the first two columns are a few dots stacked with gaps between them, and the third is filled in.

Itchy

That is the right way to look at it — and the three panels came from the same loop with enumerate, which hands you a counter alongside each item so that panel j lands in column j. It is not the columns that matter — those are the four ages. It is what is inside a column. The first two are stripes and there is nothing wrong with them; they are telling you the truth about a discrete response. What they cannot do is be compared with a normal distribution, because they are not one and never will be, however right the model is. Notice also that the response residuals have a floor that moves: a female cannot fledge fewer than none, so her residual cannot be more negative than minus her own fitted value, and the most negative one in this file is -4.073.

Momo

So the third one is the one I plot.

Itchy

The third one is the one you plot, in every family in this book. Randomised quantile residuals are Dunn and Smyth’s idea (1996) and the sentence that makes them work is short: if the model is right, the probability it assigns below your observation is uniform, and a uniform pushed through the inverse normal is standard normal. Whatever the family. For a discrete response the probability is not a single number but an interval, so you draw uniformly inside it — hence randomised, hence a generator, hence a seed, every single time.

The picture, and what its envelope promises

Itchy

Now the plot. It is the one fig_diagnostic draws, and before anybody looks at it I am going to tell you what is on the axes, because the commonest failure in this room is reading a picture whose meaning you have assumed. The horizontal axis is the quantile a standard normal would have put at that rank. The vertical axis is the deviation from it — sorted residual minus expected quantile. So a correct model does not give you a diagonal line. It gives you a flat line at zero. This is a detrended quantile plot, a worm plot, and van Buuren and Fredriks (2001) named it that because a wrong model makes it wriggle. The band is a pointwise two-standard-error envelope on each order statistic under normality.

Toto

So five per cent of the points should fall outside it.

Itchy

That is the sentence I want to kill. Draw it first, then we will find out.

Momo

107 out of 163 are outside. That is more than half.

Itchy

It is — n_outside counted them, with the same arithmetic the plot is drawn from, and n_below counted the ones under the band. And Toto has told you it should be about five per cent, and I have told you not to believe him, and neither of us has done anything but assert. The envelope is pointwise: each ribbon is drawn at two standard errors, so the rate per point really is the normal’s own two-sigma tail and Toto has not invented it. What breaks is the step from a rate to a count, and two things break it.

Toto

The points are not independent.

Itchy

That is the first. They are the same residuals sorted from smallest to largest, one per rank, so when the curve drifts it drifts together and dozens cross at once. The second is worse: these residuals are not a fresh normal sample. They came out of a model fitted to these very data, which is the caveat Dunn and Smyth put in their own abstract — normal apart from the sampling variability in the estimated parameters. So the reference everybody carries in their head, plain normal samples, is the wrong null for a picture of a fit. The right one is the recipe this whole class is about: simulate from the fitted model, refit, recompute the residuals, count. I will print the wrong null once beside it, so you can see the size of the mistake.

# (a) the WRONG null, printed once: plain standard normal samples, no model anywhere;
# (b) the RIGHT null: simulate from the fit, REFIT the same formula, recompute the
#     residuals, count -- a thousand times over.
refit_age(y) = drm(bf(@formula(Fledglings ~ Age)), Poisson();
                   data = DataFrame(Fledglings = y, Age = females.Age))

rng_naive = MersenneTwister(10)
naive_out = [n_outside(randn(rng_naive, n_fem)) for _ in 1:1000]

rng_null = MersenneTwister(11)
ynull = simulate(pois; nsim = 1000, rng = rng_null)
null_out = Int[]; null_sd = Float64[]
for k in 1:size(ynull, 2)
    r = residuals(refit_age(ynull[:, k]); type = :quantile, rng = rng_null)
    push!(null_out, n_outside(r)); push!(null_sd, std(r))
end
q95 = quantile(null_out, 0.95)

@printf("(a) normal samples, no fit : median %.0f, mean %.2f, 95th pct %.0f  (%.0f%% of %d)\n",
        median(naive_out), mean(naive_out), quantile(naive_out, 0.95),
        100 * quantile(naive_out, 0.95) / n_fem, n_fem)
@printf("    the pointwise two-sigma rate alone predicts a mean of %.2f\n",
        2 * Distributions.cdf(Distributions.Normal(), -2) * n_fem)
@printf("(b) simulate -> refit -> residuals, outside : median %.0f, mean %.2f, 95th pct %.0f\n",
        median(null_out), mean(null_out), q95)
@printf("(b) residual SD of a correct fitted model   : mean %.4f, SD %.4f\n",
        mean(null_sd), std(null_sd))
@printf("draws of %d with at least %d points outside : %d of %d\n",
        n_fem, out_p, count(null_out .>= out_p), length(null_out))

Toto

push! is new, and so is the exclamation mark.

Itchy

The mark is a convention: a function that changes what you hand it wears one, which is why scatter! and hlines! above add to a figure that already exists. push! changes a vector by appending one value to its end. Int[] and Float64[] are empty vectors that know what they will hold; simulate hands back a thousand invented response vectors from the fit, one per column; and every pass through the loop fits one of them, computes its residuals, and pushes one count and one spread. That is the loop form of a comprehension, for a body that does several things; the comprehension above it, with an underscore for a counter whose value is never wanted, is the same idea for a body that does one. And refit_age(y) = ... defines a function in one line: hand it a response vector and it fits the same formula to it, with the real ages beside.

Momo

Which line do I read first?

Itchy

The wrong one, because it is the one everybody’s intuition draws. Plain normal samples: a mean of 6.86 outside against the 7.42 the pointwise rate predicts — so Toto was very nearly right on average, and useless about any one picture, because the ninety-fifth percentile is 37, 23% of the sample. That long tail is the correlation made countable: the whole worm is free to sit above or below zero, and when it does, everything crosses at once. Now the null that applies to the picture on the screen. Fit the model to each invented world and that freedom disappears, because the intercept is estimated from the very numbers it is then judged against — the worm is pinned through the middle by construction. Median 0, ninety-fifth percentile 8. A correct model, fitted, puts almost nothing outside this band. The null everybody draws is too generous by a factor of about 5, because it answers a different question: it calibrates a picture of data, and this is a picture of a fit.

Toto

Then most of the worm plots I have squinted at were not fine.

Itchy

Most of them were not, and you had no way of knowing which. Which is why the verdict on Momo’s plot is not “it looks bad”. It is that 107 points outside turned up in 0 of 1000 correct-model draws — and none is not never: with a thousand draws and no hits the most you may say is “smaller than about three in a thousand”.

Momo

And the spread of the residuals is 1.307 when it should be one.

Itchy

And the same thousand draws say a correct model’s residual SD wanders only about 0.055 either side of one, so that is far out. Now do not make the mistake I have watched a hundred people make and count the spread, the count outside and the tilt as three pieces of evidence. One thing is wrong with these residuals, they are too widely spread; the tilt, the two opposite-sign excursions and the count outside are that one thing seen three ways. But there is one more thing to check before you believe any of them, and it is the thing almost nobody checks.

Toto

The seed.

Itchy

The seed. You handed a random generator to a residual function, so a different seed gives a different residual vector from the same fit: at seed 2 instead of seed 1 the SD is 1.269 rather than 1.307 and the count outside is 92 rather than 107 — the digits move with the seed and the verdict does not, which is the form a verdict has to take: report one seed and name it, or report a range, and never a third decimal from a single draw. What a seed does and does not promise gets a room of its own in Appendix A.

Ask the model what it believes

Itchy

Now the part I actually brought you here for, and Eddie has arrived at the right moment. Everything so far has been the model marking its own homework: residuals are computed inside the fit. Eddie.

Eddie

So how do I catch it believing something absurd, rather than fitting badly?

Itchy

You make it invent a world and you look at the world. That is the whole idea. Simulate from the fitted model with an explicit generator, recompute a statistic you care about, and see where the observed value falls in the simulated pile. The recipe is three lines and it works for every model in this book. The art is entirely in choosing the statistic, so choose one that is a question about your animal.

Momo

Then I will use the one I already know the answer to, to see whether the recipe finds it. Is it inventing females who fledge more chicks than they laid eggs?

Itchy

Testing a new tool on a case you have already settled is exactly the right instinct, and it is a superb statistic besides: it is checkable in the world, and the model has never been told it is impossible. Your file has the eggs in the second column, and the model has never seen that column.

Toto

I could not read that line.

Itchy

Three new things in it. worm_parts hands back the three columns the plot is drawn from, as named fields you read with a dot, the way you read a column of a data frame: theo is the normal quantile on the horizontal axis, dev the deviation on the vertical, se the half-width of the band. .& is and, element by element, so each point is asked two questions at once — below the band, and left of zero — and count counts the points that answer yes to both. So: 7 of the 13 points outside are below the band, and 7 of those sit at negative theoretical quantiles, the bottom of the distribution — which, for a count, is where the zeros live.

Momo

Then what is it getting wrong? The worm plot will not tell me.

Itchy

No, but the recipe will. Count the zeros.

big_sim = mean([count(>=(7), ysim2[:, k]) for k in 1:size(ysim2, 2)])

Momo

The zeros came out of the middle.

Itchy

Out of the middle, and from nowhere else. Broods of one to three chicks: 56 in the file against 83.2 in a typical world. Broods of seven or more: 13 against 14.5 — the top of the distribution is exactly where the model put it. And the broods that fledged anything at all have mean 3.93 and variance 3.81, a variance sitting on its mean, which is the Poisson’s own signature. Now the story everybody reaches for first, so that you can see why it is wrong here. Variation between females the model is not allowed to know about — some birds simply worse at this than others — would put extra zeros on the page, but it could never put them there alone: a Poisson averaged over females who differ spreads out at both ends, more zeros and more big broods together, with the variance above the mean everywhere. That extra spread is precisely what a negative binomial buys, and Class 4 has already priced it on this mean model: θ is 29.2 here, the same number Class 4 printed, the family telling you there is no extra spread to be had. So this is not a mixed Poisson, and a random effect for the female — Class 6 — is not the repair: it would buy the spread this file does not show and leave the zeros where they are.

Toto

Then what makes a zero?

Itchy

A brood that failed whole. 30 females fledged nothing and the rest fledged a Poisson number, and those are two processes — whether the nest was lost, to a predator or a storm or desertion, and how many chicks survived when it was not — that one count family cannot write. The family that can is a count model with a separate zero process, a zero-inflated or a hurdle model, and the engine at this version has no such family built in (Where the engine stops). The other repair is a column: a record of whether the nest was lost would let you fit the two processes one at a time, with families you already own, and FemaleSuccess.csv does not have it. That is the honest ending of a diagnostic — not “the model is fine”, not “the model is wrong”, but “here is the thing it does not believe, here is what the pattern points at, and here is what you would need to fit it”. And that is where this hour stops: one model, interrogated on its own. Everything on this page — residuals computed inside the fit, worlds it invented, a statistic that was a question about the bird — needed no second model in the room. Next hour, Class 5b, puts one there and asks which to prefer, and Eddie has been waiting all hour to ask for the number.

Summary

Stats stuff

  • Three residuals, one of which travels. Response: observed minus fitted. Pearson: that divided by the family’s standard deviation for the row, √V(μ̂). Randomised quantile: the model’s own cumulative probability at the observation, pushed through the inverse normal. In a Gaussian model all three are the same vector up to units, which is why “residual” feels like one word, and why habits learned there do not transfer.
  • Only quantile residuals are standard normal under a correct model, whatever the family (Dunn & Smyth 1996). Response and Pearson residuals of a count fall on a lattice however right the model is.
  • The randomisation is real and has to be reported. Two seeds on the same broken fit put 107 and 92 points outside the envelope: the digits move, the verdict does not. Report one seed and name it, or report a range. A verdict that changes with the seed is not a verdict.
  • A worm plot is a detrended quantile plot: a correct model is a flat line at zero, not a diagonal (van Buuren & Fredriks 2001). A tilt, a pair of opposite-sign excursions and a wide residual spread are one finding, not three.
  • Calibrate a diagnostic against its own null, and the null must include the fitting. Plain normal samples put a ninety-fifth percentile of 37 points outside the envelope; simulating from the fit and refitting gives 8. The null everybody’s intuition draws is too generous by a factor of about 5, because estimating the parameters removes the freedom of the whole worm to shift — quantile residuals are normal apart from the sampling variability in the estimated parameters (Dunn & Smyth 1996).
  • Simulation-based checking. Simulate from the fitted model with an explicit generator, recompute a statistic that is a question about your organism, and place the observed value in the simulated distribution. It is the only tool here that can catch a model believing something impossible, because it looks at data the model invents rather than at data it was fitted to. It is the frequentist relative of the posterior predictive check (Gelman, Meng & Stern 1996). When the statistic depends on a fit, refit each invented world before scoring it.
  • A check you pass is much weaker than a check you fail, shown on one model: the repaired fit passes the impossible-bird check and is convicted by the number of zeros, which not one of a thousand simulated worlds reached. A model usually clears the check that motivated its last repair; a statistic chosen as a fresh fact about your organism is the one that can still fail.
  • A diagnostic ends by naming what the model does not believe, and where the pattern points. The extra zeros here came out of the middle of the distribution, not the top: broods of seven or more matched the simulated worlds (13 against 14.5) and the broods that fledged anything were Poisson-shaped (mean 3.93, variance 3.81). Variation between females would have inflated the top and the variance along with the zeros, and Class 4’s negative binomial on the same mean model, θ = 29.2, had already found no such spread — so a female-level random effect (Class 6) is not the repair. Whole broods failed: the repair is a count family with a separate zero process, which the engine does not offer, or a column recording nest fate, which FemaleSuccess.csv lacks. Sometimes the repair is a different family; sometimes it is a different file.
  • Checking is not comparing. Everything on this page was one model against itself. Putting a second model beside it is Class 5b.

Julia you used

  • Int[], Float64[] and push!. An empty vector that knows its type, grown one value at a time inside a loop: the loop form of a comprehension, for a body that does more than one thing. The ! marks a function that changes what it is handed, as scatter! and hlines! change a figure.
  • refit_age(y) = drm(...). A function defined in one line, called later by name.
  • for (nm, r) in (("response", r1), ("Pearson", r2)). Looping over a list of pairs, each unpacked into two names. enumerate(list) adds a counter: for (j, (nm, r)) in enumerate(panels).
  • [... for _ in 1:1000]. An underscore for a counter whose value is never used.
  • extrema(mu_p).... The splat: spreads a pair into two separate arguments.
  • w2.theo, w2.dev, w2.se. Named fields of a result, read with a dot as a column of a data frame is. The worm-plot arithmetic that fills them is not on this page: worm_parts, n_outside and n_below live in tools/figures.jl beside fig_diagnostic, and the chapter calls them.
  • .&. And, element by element: two yes-or-no vectors in, one out.
  • -0.5:1:16.5. A range with a step: from, step, to.
  • maximum(abs, v), count(==(0), v), count(>=(7), v). A function in the first slot is applied to each element first; count(x -> 1 <= x <= 3, v) is the same with Class 1’s anonymous function in that slot.

Calls you used

  • residuals(fit; type = :response) and residuals(fit; type = :quantile, rng = MersenneTwister(seed)): observed minus fitted, and randomised quantile residuals. Pass the rng. There is no Pearson type: write (y .- fitted(fit)) ./ sqrt.(V.(fitted(fit))) yourself, with V the family’s variance function.
  • simulate(fit; nsim = k, rng = MersenneTwister(seed)): an nobs × k matrix of new responses in the family’s own support.
  • fig_diagnostic(resid), worm_parts(r), n_outside(r) and n_below(r) from tools/figures.jl: the worm plot, and the arithmetic that lets you count what it shows rather than just look at it.
  • The simulation thread. Every chapter asks the fit to invent data. This hour’s thread ran in the middle, three times: a thousand worlds from the age-only model to calibrate the worm plot’s own null, a thousand more from it to ask what it believed about the ceiling, and a thousand from the repaired model to ask about the ceiling, then the zeros, then the shape of everything that was not a zero. Appendix A takes the same move one step further and asks whether a reported standard error predicts the spread of the refits.

Further reading

Graded by depth. Details checked on 2026-09-07 against OpenAlex, an open catalogue of research papers.

  1. Dunn, P. K. & Smyth, G. K. (1996) “Randomized quantile residuals”, Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics 5:236–244. doi:10.1080/10618600.1996.10474708. Start here. The source of the one residual in this class that works in every family; the randomisation step for discrete responses — the reason a seed is involved at all — is set out there and nowhere shorter.
  2. van Buuren, S. & Fredriks, M. (2001) “Worm plot: a simple diagnostic device for modelling growth reference curves”, Statistics in Medicine 20:1259–1277. doi:10.1002/sim.746. The plot this class draws twice, in the setting it was invented for. Read it for the reading of the shape: offset, slope and curvature each answer to a different defect in the model.
  3. Gelman, A., Meng, X.-L. & Stern, H. (1996) “Posterior predictive assessment of model fitness via realized discrepancies”, Statistica Sinica 6:733–760. The origin of the idea in this chapter’s second half. A Bayesian paper in a frequentist book, so read it for the idea and not for the reference distribution.
