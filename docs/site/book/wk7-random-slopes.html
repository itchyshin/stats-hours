Not everyone shares a slope

A covariate that was two covariates, one term with two parameters, a correlation that changes sign when you move the origin without changing the model, and what the slope variance would have absorbed had the fixed part not been split first.

Class 6 used tarsus as the predictor and freed the intercept. Today the predictor is mass, the column that changes between one capture of a bird and the next, and we free the slope.

Objectives

By the end of this class you should be able to:

  1. Say what a random slope frees, and check before you fit it whether your covariate varies enough within groups for the question to have an answer.
  2. Split a repeated-measures covariate into its within and between parts, say what one coefficient on the unsplit covariate blends, and why this comes before the random slope.
  3. Fit (1 + x | group) on the within part and read σ_0, σ_1 and ρ out of the covariance block.
  4. Explain why ρ changes, and can change sign, when you move the origin of the covariate without changing the model.
  5. Compare the two models with AIC and a likelihood-ratio test, and say why the usual reference distribution is wrong when the thing tested is a variance.
  6. Say what shrinkage does to a group’s slope, and why three measurements keep only a little of the slope they suggest.

A repeatability is the share of variance that sits between individuals, so one minus it is the share within. The cell loops over five columns and stores what it finds in two lookup tables: Dict{Symbol,Float64}() is an empty Dict that will hold a number under each column’s name, filled by share_within[c] = .... Itchy

It is, and I am glad you read the whole column, because that is the trap in this table. A share does not tell you what the variation is made of. Itchy

Itchy

Itchy

Freeing the slope

μ_ij = (β0 + u_0j) + (β1 + u_1j) x_ij + β2 m_j, with (u_0j, u_1j) ~ N(0, Σ) and y_ij ~ N(μ_ij, σ²)

Itchy

Measurement i on bird j. x_ij is wdev, the capture’s departure from its own bird’s average mass; m_j is wbar, that average, and β2 the between-bird slope you just read. The rest is Class 6 with two additions. u_0j is the bird’s own nudge to the intercept, exactly last week’s u_j. u_1j is the bird’s own nudge to the slope, and it is new. They are drawn together, from a two-dimensional normal, so Σ holds three numbers rather than one: σ_0 the spread of intercepts, σ_1 the spread of slopes, and ρ the correlation between a bird’s intercept and its slope.

Eddie

Three numbers, and the model gained two parameters rather than one.

Itchy

Two: σ_1 and ρ, and that is what the test will test. In code it is one term, and Toto has already typed it.

One model, written four ways

Itchy

Fit the same model at four origins, changing nothing else. wdev is zero at each bird’s own average mass; move that zero five grams down, one gram down, leave it, move it five grams up. at_origin does one fit and ends with return and a NamedTuple, the explicit way of saying what a function hands back; map(at_origin, origins) calls it on each origin and collects the four results, a comprehension by another name.

function at_origin(origin)
    d = DataFrame(BirdID = sparrows.BirdID, Wing = sparrows.Wing, wbar = sparrows.wbar,
                  x = sparrows.wdev .- origin)
    f = drm(bf(@formula(Wing ~ x + wbar + (1 + x|BirdID))), Gaussian(); data = d)
    W = vc(f)[:BirdID]
    s0, s1 = sqrt(W[1, 1]), sqrt(W[2, 2])
    return (; sd0 = s0, sd1 = s1, rho = W[1, 2] / (s0 * s1),
            sigma = first(sigma(f)), aic = aic(f))
end

origins = [-5.0, -1.0, 0.0, 5.0]
@printf("%-14s %9s %9s %9s %9s %12s\n",

Momo

Then what is ρ about?

Itchy

ρ is the correlation between a bird’s slope and its intercept, and its intercept is its predicted wing length at x equal to zero. Move zero and you are asking about the bird’s wing length at a different mass, so of course the correlation changes. Five grams below a bird’s own average is lighter than that bird was ever weighed, so “intercept” there is an extrapolation backwards, and a bird with a steeper slope necessarily extrapolates lower — a negative correlation, manufactured by the arithmetic. Five grams above, the tilt goes the other way. At a bird’s own average, ρ is closest to being about the birds and not the origin, and that is the origin wdev gives every bird for free. So: report ρ with its origin in the same sentence, and centre a covariate whose zero is outside its range, every time.

Toto

Show me the sign change instead of making me read it off a column.

Itchy

Fair — ρ is a function of one number, where you put zero, and that is a curve, not four fits. Shift the origin by t and each bird’s intercept becomes its old intercept plus t times its slope, so the board’s Σ moves like this, with nothing refitted:

u_0j’ = u_0j + t u_1j, so Var(u_0’) = σ_0² + 2t ρσ_0σ_1 + t²σ_1², Cov(u_0’, u_1) = ρσ_0σ_1 + tσ_1², and σ_1 does not move.

rho_at(t) = (V[1, 2] + t * V[2, 2]) /

Itchy

A within-bird statement, because the column it sits on is the within column. Hold it lightly all the same. Fit the correlation because leaving it out constrains the model; do not build a story on it.

Jaro

(from the doorway) You want the mixture. Self and Liang (1987) worked out what the likelihood-ratio statistic does when the null sits on the boundary of the parameter space; Stram and Lee (1994) applied it to the mixed model, for exactly the step you have just taken — q random effects against q plus one, and here q is one. Of the two parameters you added, the variance sits on a boundary and the correlation does not, and when the variance goes to zero the correlation stops existing at all. For that case the null is a fifty-fifty mixture of chi-squared with one degree of freedom and chi-squared with two. One line of arithmetic.

Summary

Stats stuff

  • What a random slope frees. (1 + x | g) lets each group have its own steepness as well as its own height; the group lines stop being parallel and cross.
  • Check the covariate before you fit. A random slope needs x to vary within groups; one minus the repeatability of x is the share that does, and what that variation is matters as much as its size: bill width moves because the calipers do, mass because the bird does.
  • A coefficient on a repeated-measures covariate blends two slopes, and splitting them costs one parameter. Here between-bird 0.583, within-bird 0.049, and the split bought more AIC for one parameter than the random slope for two. Fit the decomposition first; then ask whether the within slope varies.
  • Standard errors can go either way. Adding (1 | g) stops counting repeated measurements as fresh evidence, which raises the standard error, and removes noise from the residual, which lowers it; here the second won.
  • Two parameters, not one. A random slope adds a variance and a covariance, and the comparison must charge for both.
  • ρ is a statement about where zero is. Move the origin of x and ρ changes, and can change sign, while the likelihood, the AIC, σ, σ_1 and the fixed slope do not move. Report ρ with the origin in the same sentence, and centre a covariate whose zero is outside its range, every time: a far-away origin can crash the optimiser without changing the model.
  • Testing σ_1 = 0 is a boundary problem. The naive χ²(2) is conservative. Stram and Lee (1994) cover exactly this step, q random effects against q + 1: a fifty-fifty mixture of χ²(1) and χ²(2). Read the assumptions of any correction before you take its p-value.
  • Shrinkage, now for slopes. A group’s predicted slope is pulled hard towards the population slope when the group has few measurements or little spread in x; here groups keep about 14% of their own slope, and the spread of the predictions is not σ_1.
  • Conditional residuals, and the rows that set the scale. fitted and residuals are population-level; subtract both of the group’s own numbers, read the worm plot for shape and not for σ, name the rows far from their own group before standardising, and say what moves with them out — here ρ went to the edge of its range.
  • A random slope on an unsplit covariate does two jobs. Its variance absorbs the between-versus-within contrast the fixed part refused to separate; on the blended column σ_1 would have been 0.431 rather than 0.254, and 60% of the likelihood-ratio evidence would have been misspecification — Bates et al. (2015) against Barr et al. (2013), in one file.

  • leftjoin(df, other, on = :BirdID) and indexin(a, b). The join attaches the columns of other to every row of df whose key matches; indexin gives each element of a its position in b. The two lookups you no longer build by hand.
  • nothing as a cell’s last line. Stops the cell printing a value nobody needs to see.
  • Dict{Symbol,Float64}() and d[k] = v. An empty lookup table with declared key and value types, filled one entry at a time.
  • :y => (v -> cond ? a : missing) => :sw. An anonymous function inside combine, and missing as a value a later skipmissing steps over.
  • V[1, 2] and B[:, 2]. Row one, column two of a matrix; the whole second column.
  • return (; sd0 = s0, ...) and map(f, xs). An explicit return of a NamedTuple, and a function applied to each element of a list with the results collected.
  • findall(v), setdiff(1:n, bad), df[keep, :]. The positions where a true/false vector is true, one set of positions with another removed, and the rows at those positions.

Calls you used

  • @formula(y ~ x + (1 + x|g)): the random slope. Everything else about the call is unchanged from Class 6.
  • vc(fit)[:g]: the 2 × 2 covariance of a correlated block; the diagonal’s square roots are σ₀ and σ₁, the off-diagonal over their product is ρ. Why re_sd is empty here is in Where the engine stops.
  • ranef(fit)[:g]: intercept deviations in column one, slope deviations in column two, in the order of sort(unique(df.g)).
  • aic(fit), loglik(fit), dof(fit), lrtest(reduced, full): the comparison and the naive test, which warns when the extra parameters include a variance. The Stram and Lee mixture is one line: 0.5 * ccdf(Chisq(1), s) + 0.5 * ccdf(Chisq(2), s).
