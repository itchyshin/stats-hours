Objectives

By the end of this class you should be able to:

  1. State, in one sentence, what a family decrees about the variance, and compare a crude variance-to-mean ratio against the ratio the fitted model actually implies, rather than against one.
  2. Look for an exposure — a denominator already sitting in your data — before you blame the family, and say what an offset is.
  3. Fit a grouped binomial and its overdispersed cousin BetaBinomial(), and say why n·p(1 − p) is a claim data can break where p(1 − p) is not.
  4. Fit NegBinomial2(), say what the dispersion parameter θ means in the units of the variance, and predict what admitting the extra spread will and will not change: the estimate barely, the standard errors a lot.
  5. Judge an overdispersion check against its own error bar, and a ±1 SD band against the coverage — the share of data such a band is meant to hold — the model itself claims for it.
  6. Put a second formula on σ in a Gaussian model, read the null hypothesis printed above the σ table, and report a not detected honestly — with the smallest difference the design could have seen. Then say why that is the same move as changing the family, and name the rung where it gets a chapter of its own.

Momo

Itchy

By what you are asking, not by AIC. If the biology is about production — how many chicks come out of this female — model the count and put the eggs in as an exposure. If it is about success rate given the attempt, model the proportion, and then the ceiling is built in rather than negotiated. A band is not a promise that the dots are inside it

Itchy

Everybody draws mean ± 1 SD and then says “look, the dots are inside”. Count them instead, and count them against what the model itself says the answer should be.

# How many birds sit outside their own model's mean ± 1 SD, and how often does the
# model itself expect a bird to sit inside? (Sum the fitted pmf over the band.)
outside(y, m, v) = count(abs.(y .- m) .> sqrt.(v))

# The ±2SE envelope fig_diagnostic draws, reproduced here so that what we COUNT is
# exactly what the figure SHOWS. erfinv_approx comes from tools/figures.jl.
function worm_outside(q)
    m = length(q)
    p = ((1:m) .- 0.5) ./ m
    theo = sqrt(2) .* erfinv_approx.(2 .* p .- 1)
    dev = sort(q) .- theo
    se = sqrt.(p .* (1 .- p) ./ m) ./ (exp.(-theo .^ 2 ./ 2) ./ sqrt(2π))
    out = abs.(dev) .> 2 .* se
    longest = 0; run_len = 0; sgn = 0
    for i in 1:m
        if out[i]
            run_len = (run_len > 0 && sign(dev[i]) == sgn) ? run_len + 1 : 1
            sgn = sign(dev[i])
        else
            run_len = 0; sgn = 0
        end
        longest = max(longest, run_len)
    end
    idx = findall(out)
    span = isempty(idx) ? (NaN, NaN) : (minimum(theo[idx]), maximum(theo[idx]))
    (out = count(out), longest = longest, lo = span[1], hi = span[2],
     neg_left = count(dev[1:div(m, 3)] .< 0), third = div(m, 3))
end

Itchy

Same first line, same second line, and a third line with a free parameter in it. θ is estimated from the data like any other parameter, and it buys the variance permission to exceed the mean. Summary

Stats stuff

  • Compare like with like. A Poisson regression decrees Var(y | x) = μ(x); it says nothing about the variance-to-mean ratio of the raw counts, which also carries the variation the predictors create. Here the raw ratio is 1.69 and a perfectly correct Poisson would still imply 1.15. Check overdispersion on residuals, not on the marginal moments.
  • Look for an exposure before you blame the family. An offset or denominator sitting unused in your data is the commonest cause of “overdispersion” and the cheapest to fix. Here log(EggNo) bought about 87 AIC for one parameter and moved the age rate ratio from 1.26 to 1.09, interval covering one. A missing covariate and a wrong variance function look identical in a residual plot.
  • An offset is a covariate with its coefficient pinned at one. Fit it free first: if the interval covers one, the offset is the better model; if it does not, the offset would have been a confident mistake.
  • Successes out of trials is not a count. For n > 1 the binomial variance is n·p(1 − p), which is a real claim — the trials within a row are independent — and data can break it. For n = 1 it is an identity, which is why Class 3’s logistic diagnostic could not fail.
  • The beta-binomial frees that claim with one parameter, as the negative binomial frees Var = μ with θ. Here it bought about 25 AIC and took the Pearson statistic from 1.64 to 1.02.
  • AIC compares models of the same response. Counts and proportions-out-of-a-denominator are different data. Choose between them by the question you are asking, not by the smaller number.
  • Overdispersion is a disease of standard errors, and that is a theorem. The Poisson score equations are unbiased estimating equations for β whatever the true variance, so the point estimate stays consistent; only its standard error was wrong. Here it was wrong by a factor of 1.39, against the quasi-Poisson’s 1.25 — close, not identical, because the two weight observations differently.
  • Read the null the engine prints above the σ table, then check it applies to your family. For a Gaussian, σ = 1 is unit-dependent and rarely interesting. For NegBinomial2() and BetaBinomial(), σ is dimensionless and σ = 1 is a real dispersion hypothesis that the printed z genuinely tests.
  • Judge a band against its own claim. A ±1 SD band is not supposed to contain the data. Ask the model what coverage it claims and compare: 0.53 against 0.67 for the binomial, 0.66 against 0.67 for the beta-binomial.
  • “Not detected” is not “no”. The sparrows’ σ ratio is 1.06 with an interval of 0.85 to 1.31, and the smallest ratio this design could have caught at 80% power is 1.35. Report the interval and the detectable size, not a verdict.
  • A model comparison that goes against you is a result. The counts earned their beta-binomial parameter and the sparrows lost their σ term by about 1.7 AIC. Reporting only the one that worked is how a literature goes wrong.
  • Plot the assumption, not only its consequences. Mean against variance, binned, with the family’s claim drawn as a curve and marker area showing sample size, is the fastest way to see a variance assumption fail or hold.
  • This chapter still pretends rows are independent, and these files cannot stop it. FemaleSuccess.csv carries no female identifier at all; MBodySize.csv does carry BirdID, but one row per bird, so there is nothing for a random intercept to group. The repeated-measures file is BodySize.csv. Class 6 removes the pretence there, and Class 9 puts today’s freed variance and Class 6’s freed correlation into the same model.

Julia stuff

  • drm(bf(@formula(cbind(k, n - k) ~ x)), Binomial(); data = df): a grouped binomial. The response is two columns — successes and failures — and cbind is DRM’s own marker, not R’s function.
  • drm(bf(@formula(cbind(k, n - k) ~ x), @formula(sigma ~ 1)), BetaBinomial(); data = df): the overdispersed version. coef(fit, :sigma) is log σ and the precision is φ = 1/σ², so exp(-2 * coef(fit, :sigma)[1]).
  • drm(bf(@formula(y ~ x), @formula(sigma ~ 1)), NegBinomial2(); data = df): overdispersed counts, with θ = 1/σ² by the same arithmetic. Neither engine prints θ or φ for you.
  • There is no offset in DRM.jl 0.7.1 — a known limitation, reported to the engine’s authors. Add log(exposure) as an ordinary covariate and check whether its interval covers one; drmTMB writes offset(log(exposure)) directly.
  • drm(bf(@formula(y ~ x + g), @formula(sigma ~ g)), Gaussian(); data = df): a location-scale Gaussian fit. coef(fit, :sigma) is on the log scale; exponentiate for millimetres.
  • sigma(fit): the response-scale σ per observation for a Gaussian fit. On a constant-σ fit every element is the same number; on a modelled σ it is a range, which is what the fit header prints. On BetaBinomial() it returns the σ and the trial counts, so read θ or φ off coef(fit, :sigma) instead.
  • coeftable(fit): row names are prefixed by block, so "mu: Age" and "sigma: Sex: male". Columns 5 and 6 are the confidence limits, on the link scale, so exponentiate them for a rate ratio or a σ ratio.
  • dof_residual(fit): exported and needed for a Pearson dispersion statistic, which the engine does not compute for you.
  • residuals(fit; type = :quantile, rng = MersenneTwister(seed)): standard normal under a correct model for every family here. Pass the rng — and know that which rng you pass moves the second decimal, so quote the SD against its Monte Carlo standard error of 1/√(2n) rather than reading its digits.
  • import Distributions: needed here because DRM exports its own Poisson, Binomial and BetaBinomial. Qualify the distribution as Distributions.Poisson(μ) when you want a pmf or a cdf.
  • Marker size as sample size. markersize = 3 .* sqrt.(n) in a binned plot. Area, not radius, is what the eye reads.
  • The simulation thread. Ask the negative-binomial fit to invent two hundred new sets of females, refit each, and watch which parameter is well pinned down and which is not.
# Draw new counts from the fitted NB2 model, refit the same model to each,
# and collect two things: the slope on Age, and the dispersion theta.
rng_sim = MersenneTwister(20260907)
ysim = simulate(nb; nsim = 200, rng = rng_sim)

function refit_pair(y)
    d = DataFrame(Fledglings = y, Age = females.Age)
    f = drm(bf(@formula(Fledglings ~ Age), @formula(sigma ~ 1)),
            NegBinomial2(); data = d)
    (coef(f, :mu)[2], exp(-2 * coef(f, :sigma)[1]))
end

pairs_out = [refit_pair(ysim[:, k]) for k in 1:size(ysim, 2)]
slopes = first.(pairs_out)
thetas = last.(pairs_out)

# Monte-Carlo SE of a median, so the "bias" claim carries its own error bar.
# The textbook 1.2533 * SD / sqrt(n) is a LARGE-SAMPLE formula and runs wide for a
# statistic this skewed, so resample the refits themselves and use that instead.
rng_boot = MersenneTwister(4321)
n_ref = length(thetas)
boot_med = [median(thetas[rand(rng_boot, 1:n_ref, n_ref)]) for _ in 1:2000]
mcse_median = std(boot_med)
mcse_asymptotic = 1.2533 * std(thetas) / sqrt(n_ref)
bias_pct = 100 * (median(thetas) - theta) / theta

The slope behaves itself: the spread of refitted slopes lands close to the standard error the single fit reported, which is what a standard error promises. θ does not. Its refits are strongly asymmetric — the mean printed above sits higher than the median, the largest refit is 2.3 times the estimate, and the tenth and ninetieth percentiles are nowhere near symmetric about 3.99. The median itself is 2.7% off the generating value, which is 1.12 Monte-Carlo standard errors. Read that as two hundred refits cannot resolve a bias of a few per cent, not as there is no bias: the two sentences are different, and only the first one is what these two hundred refits measured. Note also which error bar it is measured against. The textbook Monte-Carlo standard error of a median, 1.2533 × SD/√n, is a large-sample approximation, and on a distribution this skewed it comes out at 0.11 where resampling the refits themselves gives 0.097 — so the formula understates the evidence by being too generous with the error bar, which is a nice inversion of the usual complaint. The asymmetry, though, is unmistakable, and it is why an interval read off the likelihood itself beats the plain estimate-plus-or-minus-two-standard-errors kind — the Wald interval, which assumes the uncertainty is the same size on both sides — for this parameter, and why quoting a dispersion to three decimal places is theatre. Appendix A takes that apart properly.

Further reading

  1. Ver Hoef, J. M. & Boveng, P. L. (2007) “Quasi-Poisson vs. negative binomial regression: how should we model overdispersed count data?”, Ecology 88:2766–2772. doi:10.1890/07-0043.1. Start here, and read it before you choose. It sets the negative binomial against the other standard repair, the quasi-Poisson, and the difference is not cosmetic: they weight observations differently, so they can disagree about which coefficient matters. This class computed both inflation factors in one line; the paper is why they are not the same number.
  2. Lindén, A. & Mäntyniemi, S. (2011) “Using the negative binomial distribution to model overdispersion in ecological count data”, Ecology 92:1414–1421. doi:10.1890/10-1831.1. The ecologist’s companion to the equation on the board, and the paper that would have caught this chapter’s first mistake: its abstract is about the mechanisms that generate overdispersion — sampling, flocking, aggregation, environmental variability — which is exactly the question “is the family wrong, or is something missing from the mean?”.
  3. Harrison, X. A. (2014) “Using observation-level random effects to model overdispersion in count data in ecology and evolution”, PeerJ 2:e616. doi:10.7717/peerj.616. The repair this chapter deliberately did not use, because it needs machinery from Class 6. Read it after Class 6 and then come back: it is a third route to the same problem, and knowing three routes is what stops you believing any one of them is the definition of the problem.

Exercises

Use your own organism where one is named. For every question, paste your code and then explain in your own words what each line does, as if to somebody who has done Class 3 and not Class 4.

  1. Read the file before the model. Take any count response of your own and list every other column. For each one, answer in a sentence: is this a predictor, an exposure, or neither? An exposure is anything that changes how much opportunity a row had — area searched, hours observed, eggs laid, individuals at risk. If you find one, you have found this chapter’s first lesson in your own data.

  2. Find the lie, honestly. Report the variance-to-mean ratio of your raw counts. Then fit a Poisson model and report the Pearson X²/df and the standard deviation of residuals(fit; type = :quantile, rng = MersenneTwister(1)) against its Monte Carlo standard error of 1/√(2n). Say which of the three you would put in a paper, and why the other two are still worth having computed.

  3. The wrong benchmark. From your fitted Poisson, compute (mean(fitted(fit)) + var(fitted(fit))) / mean(fitted(fit)) — the marginal variance-to-mean ratio a correct Poisson regression implies for your data. Compare it with the raw ratio from question 2 and with one. Then write two sentences: one naming which of the two comparisons is like-for-like, and one saying how much of your apparent overdispersion survives it.

  4. Pay the exposure first. If question 1 found an exposure, add log(exposure) to the mean model. Report both AICs, the coefficient on log(exposure) with its interval, and whether that interval covers one. Then report what happened to the coefficient you actually care about, and say in one sentence whether you would now write a different result.

  5. Pay the price. Refit with NegBinomial2(), with and without the exposure. Report the coefficient, the standard error and the rate ratio in a four-row table, and report θ in both. Then write one sentence naming what changed and one naming what did not.

  6. Out of how many? If your response is a success count out of a known denominator, refit it as cbind(successes, failures) with Binomial() and then BetaBinomial(). Report both AICs, the Pearson X²/df under each, and φ. Then answer in one sentence why you may not compare either AIC with the count models from question 5.

  7. The referee. Report the AIC difference for the repair you kept. Then answer: if it had gone the other way, what would you have done, and would you have reported the comparison at all? Two sentences, and be honest in the second one.

  8. Momo’s tadpoles. Count something per pond and fit both families. Draw the mean-against-variance figure from this class, binning by a predictor of your choice, with marker area showing bin sample size. Name the bin you distrust most, say why, and give its n.

  9. Eddie’s dunnocks. Fit Wing ~ Tarsus on Eddie’s data, then add @formula(sigma ~ Sex) to the bf. Report both AICs, the z and p on the σ slope row, the σ ratio with its interval, and the smallest ratio your sample size could have detected at 80% power (exp((1.96 + 0.84) * se)). Then write the result as a “not detected, and we could only have seen X” sentence rather than a “no”.

  10. Count the band. Take any fit from this class’s exercises, draw its mean ± 1 SD band, and count how many of your observations fall outside it. Then ask the fitted distribution what fraction it expects to fall inside. Report both. One sentence on which of the two numbers a reader can interpret without the other.

  11. The two faces. Write one paragraph, no code, explaining to a labmate why BetaBinomial(), NegBinomial2() and sigma ~ x are the same move. Your paragraph must contain the phrase “held fixed” and must name what is held fixed in each case.

  12. Simulate and watch it wander. Adapt this class’s last block of code to your own overdispersed fit, with your own seed.
