Caveat A preface describes a book that is not finished. The climb is twelve rungs and a coda; ten of the rungs, plus this preface and the coda, make up version 1, and all ten are written and their code runs. Where this page says what a rung does, it is describing the plan for the whole climb (shown below, and linked from the landing page), not necessarily a chapter you can read today.

This book is for a student — undergraduate or graduate — learning statistics and Julia at the same time. You may have used R a little. You have never opened Julia. Nobody expects you to have fitted a mixed model, or to know what one is. Nothing on these pages assumes more than that: basic Julia is Class 1, taught on the same data you model in Class 2, and every statistical word arrives at the moment a model needs it. If that is you, you are the reader, and the climb is paced for you.

Here is the kind of question it answers. The house sparrows of Lundy Island have been caught, measured, ringed and followed, nest by nest, for years, and the files from those years are the data of this book. Does a bigger-boned bird carry a longer wing, and by how much? Why do some chicks die in the nest while others fledge, and why does one female fledge more than another? When the same bird turns up in the file four times, have you learned something four times, or once? And how much of what a chick becomes did it get from its parents, and how much from the nest it grew up in? Every one of those is a case of the sentence Class 2 opens on: things vary, and some of the variation has a reason. A statistical model is the instrument for saying how much of the variation has a reason, what the reason is, and how sure you are — and for being told, by the numbers, when you have claimed too much. That is what this book teaches, on those birds, one question at a time: the wing in Class 2, the chicks in Class 3, the bird measured four times in Class 6, the parents and the nest in Class 10. The rungs between are what it takes to get from each of those questions to the next one honestly.

It is a reboot of Statistical Models with R: An Introduction with Sparrows, drafted in 2009–2010 (the recovered files are dated November 2009 to March 2010) and never published. What survives from that draft is the shape: an office hour rather than a lecture, a small cast who each own an organism, real birds off a real island, and the habit of leaving the mistakes in. What changed is the engine and the language, and the fact that every number in this version comes from code that ran, not from a keyboard.

How to read it

The climb. The book is not organised by topic. It is organised by constants. Each rung frees one thing the rung before it held fixed: the shape of the noise, then the spread, then the correlation between rows, then the slope, then the assumption that groups are independent of one another. Toto’s mistakes are not decorative. They are the mistakes people actually make, in the order people actually make them, and the correction is usually the point of the section.

The errors stay in. When a piece of code fails in this book, it fails on the page: a real Julia error, raised by the real engine, printed where it happened. Nothing is a screenshot of a failure. Class 1 breaks things on purpose four times, because reading the message afterwards is the skill that decides whether a reader new to the language stays or bounces, and there is a literature saying so plainly (Barik et al. 2017, cited in that chapter).

Two threads run the length of the climb, and they are not the same kind of thing.

The first is Julia itself. Basic Julia is not an appendix and not a prerequisite — it is Class 1, taught with the same data you model in Class 2.

The second is simulation. From Class 2 onward, most chapters end their Julia summary with one short block of code that acts out what the model claims: draw new data from the fitted model, refit it, and watch the estimate wander (Class 8 carries its own in the body of the hour instead, and Class 7 hands the job to Class 8). It is not a rung, because a rung frees a constant and this is a way of thinking rather than a model; Appendix A — simulation as a way of thinking gathers the whole thread in one place: from making random numbers repeatable, to checking whether an interval really catches the truth as often as it claims, to re-fitting a model on data it generated itself.

The exercises are applied and dataset-driven, and every one of them asks you to explain your code in your own words, to somebody who has not opened Julia. That instruction is the exercise.

The data are real. They are 171 house sparrows measured on Lundy Island for that course, plus the repeated-measures, survival and breeding files that came with them; where they came from, how many rows each file has, and how to check the files have not changed are written down in the notes that travel with the data. Where a chapter needs a structure those files do not contain, it makes up data from a stated random-number seed — so the same numbers appear every time — and says so at the top of the page, in the Caveat line, before you read a number.

You can also run the chapters without installing anything. Each chapter exists as a notebook you can open in a browser, and its first block installs the engine. How long that first install takes has so far only been timed on the author’s own computer, not in the browser; the notes on the reader’s set-up say so plainly.

What this book will not cover

Meta-analysis and phylogenetic comparative methods. Meta-analysis combines the results of many published studies; phylogenetic comparative methods compare species while allowing for their shared ancestry. Both are mentioned. Neither gets a chapter, and the omission is deliberate rather than an oversight: they are the same statistical problem wearing different clothes — non-independence that arrives when several published estimates come from the same study, and non-independence that arrives from shared ancestry — and they belong together in a second book rather than split across the back of this one.

Read that fence precisely. Class 10 does teach a model in which the groups are told how related they are to one another, and a species tree (a phylogeny) is one of the four forms that idea takes. What is fenced off is the field around it: the comparative question, the tree and its uncertainty, the effect-size literature, the machinery for combining published estimates. You will meet the matrix. You will not be taught the discipline that lives on it.

Rungs eleven and twelve. Version one of this book climbs to rung ten and stops. Modelling the spread itself, and two responses at once with a correlation that varies, are held for a second version. The coda says what they are and why they are the same argument continued rather than a new one.

Bayesian inference — the approach where you state what you believed before seeing the data and let the data update it. The engines do not do that; they work from the data alone. Every interval in this book is a confidence interval, and every fit is found by maximum likelihood or its close relative, REML; Class 8 is about exactly which of those two you got and were never asked to choose, and each of those words is explained at the moment a model needs it, not here. That is a scope decision, not a verdict on the alternative.

Installation. Class 1 teaches everything the language asks of you — files, data frames (tables of data), pictures, and reading a wall of red text — and skips the install. Today the two engines are installed straight from their GitHub pages, which takes a paragraph of instructions; it becomes a single line the day they are published in the official package lists. Writing that paragraph now means writing it twice.

Why one engine

It is a statistics book with one engine in it.

Not one language — there are two, R and Julia — but one engine: a single pair of twinned packages, drmTMB in R and DRM.jl in Julia, that fit every model from the first straight line to the last relatedness matrix — a table of how related every pair of animals is. Nothing is swapped out halfway. When you reach the chapter where rows stop being strangers, you do not also learn a new package, a new formula grammar and a new summary layout on the same morning.

The climb is the reason. Each rung frees one thing, and if the software changes at the same moment the model does, you cannot tell which of the two moved the number. A chapter that fits a random intercept in a new package and reports a different standard error has taught you nothing, because you cannot say whether the estimator changed, or only the printing did. Holding the engine still is what makes the model the only moving part.

There is a second reason, less tidy and more honest. The climb needs models that can allow for unequal effort or exposure per row, that let the scatter itself be modelled rather than fixed, that can be told how the groups in the data are related to one another, and that end in a heritability — all fitted the same way, from the data alone, in one frame. You will meet the name for each of those at the moment a model needs it, not here. That combination is awkward to assemble in Julia today out of the general-purpose packages, which is precisely why these two engines exist. A book that stitched five packages together would spend its teaching time on the stitching.

Other packages appear in three boxes, and nowhere else. A box has to earn its place, and the only way it can is by stopping a misreading: two correct programs print different numbers for the same model, and the box names the mechanism and gives you a boundary you can compute. There are three in the whole book. Class 2’s, where R’s lm and this engine disagree about the residual spread, and the reason is a divisor. Class 8’s, where R’s lmer and this engine disagree about a variance, and the reason is that each chose a different estimator on your behalf and neither said so. Class 9’s, where two programs approximate the same integral by different rules, and the disagreement is about neither program. Those are among the best pages in the book, and the reason there are not more of them is the reason there are not more of anything here: writing every chapter twice, in two languages, costs more attention than it returns — a finding about split attention that is older than either language and does not care which two you pick (Chandler & Sweller 1991).

Where this book sits. It is not a tour of Julia or of its data ecosystem; there are good books for that. It teaches you to fit a model for counts or yes/no outcomes measured on groups of related individuals, allow for unequal effort, know exactly which method produced each number, and be able to defend it.

Nothing here is typed in

Never invent output. Every number, every printed table, every figure on every page of this book is produced when the site is built, by running the code you can see above it. Nothing is typed from memory, nothing is pasted from a session that is not shown, and when someone in the dialogue says a number, that number was computed by the code on the page a moment earlier, not remembered and typed in.

That is the rule that does most work here, because a chapter with plausible invented numbers is worse than an obviously unfinished one: nobody catches it. So it is not a promise, it is a mechanism: the page you are reading cannot exist unless its code ran. The smallest possible demonstration of it is the colophon at the foot of this page, after the reading list, where the only code on this page runs.

Further reading

Four, no more, because a preface is not a syllabus. The details of all four were checked on 2026-09-07 against OpenAlex, an open catalogue of research papers.

  1. Bezanson, J., Edelman, A., Karpinski, S. & Shah, V. B. (2017) “Julia: A Fresh Approach to Numerical Computing”, SIAM Review 59(1):65–98. doi:10.1137/141000671. The language explained by the people who built it. Read it for one idea — that a function is a family of methods chosen by the types of all its arguments — because that idea is what makes an engine like this one possible to write, and it is also what the error messages in Class 1 are printing at you.
  2. Bolker, B. M., Brooks, M. E., Clark, C. J., Geange, S. W., Poulsen, J. R., Stevens, M. H. H. & White, J.-S. S. (2009) “Generalized linear mixed models: a practical guide for ecology and evolution”, Trends in Ecology & Evolution 24(3):127–135. doi:10.1016/j.tree.2008.10.008. The paper that showed biologists, not only statisticians, that two programs fitting the same model can print different numbers. Read it now for the map of where this book is going, and again after Class 8 for the disagreement it describes; its software advice has aged, its practice has not.
  3. Chandler, P. & Sweller, J. (1991) “Cognitive Load Theory and the Format of Instruction”, Cognition and Instruction 8(4):293–332. doi:10.1207/s1532690xci0804_2. The evidence behind the decision not to print every example twice. Splitting material a reader must mentally integrate across two places costs comprehension; that finding is why the R boxes in this book are rare and earned rather than routine.
  4. Peng, R. D. (2011) “Reproducible Research in Computational Science”, Science 334(6060):1226–1227. doi:10.1126/science.1213847. Two pages on why code and data travelling with the claim is the minimum standard, not a virtue. It is the argument this book applies to itself, and the reason the rule above is a mechanism rather than a promise.
